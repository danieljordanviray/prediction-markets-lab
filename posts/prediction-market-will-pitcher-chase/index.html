Methodology

I wanted to start simple. I purposely chose this narrow, niche market because it was the easiest to potentially model, as opposed to something much more complex like which team wins the entire game.

My thought process was that walks are mostly dependent on a pitcher’s skill level. At the professional level, I made the assumption that batters are skilled enough to easily identify balls, and that they are disciplined enough to let them go and take a walk. (This assumption is probably wrong, but it simplified the model significantly to start.)

So then now, I would just need to look at Chase Burn’s pitching record during your run-of-the-mill regular season game. He’s been in the MLB since 2025, so he has enough data points to observe and have more confidence in any statistical patterns.

So, I wanted to know the following:

  • How many walks does he usually have per game?

  • How many innings per game does he usually pitch?

  • Has he shown improvement over time? Should I weight the data from his more recent games more heavily?

And then, I wanted to make any adjustments with any qualitative factors that isn’t represented in the data (for example, coaches decisions on playing times, resting players, saving pitchers for big games coming up, etc.)

Probabilities and the Poisson distribution

Now, we have the expected number of walks for a given number of innings pitched from different cuts of data. The next question is: How do we turn this information into tangible probability figures that we can compare to the market probability of “No” at 54.22%?

This data can be modeled using a Poisson distribution.

There are three conditions for events to be modeled using a Poisson distribution:

  1. Events are independent and do not affect each other. (i.e., one batter walking doesn’t affect the chances of another batter walking.)

  2. Events must happen one a time; they cannot happen together.

  3. The frequency of an event occurring within a specific time period must be known.

On the third condition: The frequency of an event occurring within a specific time period is called lambda λ. The Poisson distribution relies only on this one parameter.

In this case, we have:

  • λ = expected number of walks (the event, y) for a specific number of innings (the period of time).

  • λ = walk rate per inning × innings pitched (The assumption here is that the walks per inning rate stays constant every inning of the game. This probably isn’t entirely true since pitchers get tired as the game progresses, but it simplifies the model.)

The probability function for the Poisson distribution is:

P(y) = (λ ^ y) * (e^-λ) / y!

And therefore, to get the probability of fewer than two walks, we must obtain:

P(<2 walks) = P(0 walks) + P(1 walk)

Result

Since my model showed the probability of Burns NOT walking 2 or more batters was > ~66% if he pitched 4 or fewer innings, while the market was trading at 54.22%, I decided to bet “No”.

During the game, he in fact only walked one batter in 2nd inning, and then was pulled out after the 3rd inning (I’m assuming due to the coach’s pitching cap). Therefore, my prediction was correct, yielding an 84% return on my bet.

However, my belief is that liquid prediction markets are quite accurate and it’s difficult to consistently find an edge for betting purposes. I think the real value from a predictive modeling perspective is using these prediction markets as a “ground-truth” to practice statistical modeling techniques.

In this case, my model was close to what the market was predicting, so it’s validation that the (simple) model was quite accurate, if not more accurate than the market.
